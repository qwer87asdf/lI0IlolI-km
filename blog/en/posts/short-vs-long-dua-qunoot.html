Short vs Long Dua Qunoot: Different Versions Explained

Shafayat · 26 Jul 2026 · 9 min read

If you’ve ever prayed Witr behind different imams, you may have noticed something: the Dua Qunoot they recite isn’t always the same length. Sometimes it’s a short, simple supplication lasting a few seconds. Other times, it’s a longer, more elaborate dua that continues for a minute or more.

This isn’t a mistake or inconsistency – it reflects genuine, authentic variations in how Dua Qunoot has been transmitted and practiced across different scholarly traditions. In this guide, we’ll break down the short and long versions of Dua Qunoot, where each comes from, and when each is typically used.

Quick Summary

  • Dua Qunoot is a supplication recited in the Witr prayer, typically in the final rak’ah after ruku (bowing) or before it, depending on the school of thought.
  • The short Dua Qunoot is the version taught by the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ to his grandson Hasan ibn Ali, and is the most widely authenticated and commonly used version.
  • The long Dua Qunoot includes additional lines of praise and supplication, drawn from various companions’ practices, and is more commonly used in certain regions and by scholars who prefer a more extended recitation.
  • Both versions are valid; the choice often comes down to madhab (school of thought), regional tradition, or personal preference within permissible limits.
  • There’s also a separate concept called Qunoot al-Nazilah – a special Dua Qunoot recited during times of calamity or hardship, distinct from the regular Witr qunoot.

What Is Dua Qunoot?

Dua Qunoot (دعاء القنوت) is a specific supplication recited by Muslims, most commonly during the Witr prayer – an odd-numbered prayer performed after Isha and considered highly recommended (sunnah mu’akkadah) in Islam.

The word “Qunoot” linguistically refers to devotion, obedience, and standing in humble submission before Allah. In practice, it refers to this specific dua recited while standing during prayer, usually in the last rak’ah of Witr.

The Short Dua Qunoot

Origin

The short version of Dua Qunoot is the most authentically documented and widely recited across the Muslim world. It was taught directly by the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ to his grandson, Hasan ibn Ali (RA), who narrated:

“The Messenger of Allah ﷺ taught me some words to say in the Witr prayer…” (Recorded in Sunan Abu Dawood, At-Tirmidhi, and other hadith collections)

Because of this strong and direct chain of transmission, the short Dua Qunoot is considered the most authentic and is the version most commonly taught in Islamic schools, mosques, and Quran memorization programs worldwide.

The Short Dua Qunoot (Arabic)

اللَّهُمَّ اهْدِنِي فِيمَنْ هَدَيْتَ، وَعَافِنِي فِيمَنْ عَافَيْتَ، وَتَوَلَّنِي فِيمَنْ تَوَلَّيْتَ، وَبَارِكْ لِي فِيمَا أَعْطَيْتَ، وَقِنِي شَرَّ مَا قَضَيْتَ، فَإِنَّكَ تَقْضِي وَلَا يُقْضَى عَلَيْكَ، وَإِنَّهُ لَا يَذِلُّ مَنْ وَالَيْتَ، تَبَارَكْتَ رَبَّنَا وَتَعَالَيْتَ

Transliteration

“Allahumma-hdini feeman hadayt, wa ‘aafini feeman ‘aafayt, wa tawallani feeman tawallayt, wa baarik lee feema a’tayt, wa qini sharra ma qadayt, fa innaka taqdi wa la yuqda ‘alayk, wa innahu la yadhillu man walayt, tabarakta rabbana wa ta’aalayt.”

English Translation

“O Allah, guide me among those You have guided, pardon me among those You have pardoned, turn to me in friendship among those on whom You have turned in friendship, and bless me in what You have bestowed, and save me from the evil of what You have decreed. For verily You decree, and none can decree against You. He whom You befriend is not humiliated. Blessed are You, our Lord, and Exalted.”

When It’s Used

This short version is used by the majority of Muslims globally during Witr prayer and is often the first version taught to children and new Muslims due to its brevity and strong authenticity.

The Long Dua Qunoot

Origin

The longer version of Dua Qunoot incorporates additional lines of praise, glorification, and supplication beyond the short version. It draws from various narrations attributed to companions such as Umar ibn al-Khattab (RA) and is commonly associated with certain regional and scholarly traditions, particularly within parts of the Hanafi madhab and some South Asian and Middle Eastern communities.

This version is longer not because it replaces the short one, but because certain traditions add extra supplications – often including additional praise of Allah, seeking forgiveness, and sending blessings upon the Prophet ﷺ – either combined with the short version or recited separately.

A Common Extended Addition

Many who recite the long Dua Qunoot include this additional portion after (or alongside) the short version:

اللَّهُمَّ إِنَّا نَسْتَعِينُكَ وَنَسْتَغْفِرُكَ وَنُثْنِي عَلَيْكَ الْخَيْرَ وَلَا نَكْفُرُكَ وَنَخْلَعُ وَنَتْرُكُ مَنْ يَفْجُرُكَ، اللَّهُمَّ إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَلَكَ نُصَلِّي وَنَسْجُدُ وَإِلَيْكَ نَسْعَى وَنَحْفِدُ، نَرْجُو رَحْمَتَكَ وَنَخْشَى عَذَابَكَ، إِنَّ عَذَابَكَ الْجِدَّ بِالْكُفَّارِ مُلْحِقٌ

Transliteration:

“Allahumma inna nasta’eenuka wa nastaghfiruka wa nuthni ‘alaykal-khayr wa la nakfuruka wa nakhla’u wa natruku man yafjuruk. Allahumma iyyaka na’budu wa laka nusalli wa nasjudu wa ilayka nas’aa wa nahfid, narju rahmataka wa nakhsha ‘adhabaka, inna ‘adhabakal-jidda bil kuffari mulhiq.”

English Translation (summary):

“O Allah, we seek Your help, seek Your forgiveness, and praise You with all goodness. We do not disbelieve in You, and we forsake and abandon whoever disobeys You. O Allah, You alone we worship, to You we pray and prostrate, and to You we hasten to serve. We hope for Your mercy and fear Your punishment, for indeed Your punishment will surely reach the disbelievers.”

When It’s Used

The long version of Dua Qunoot tends to be more common in specific regional practices and among those following certain scholarly interpretations. It’s often recited by imams in congregational Witr prayers, particularly during Ramadan, when Witr is prayed in congregation after Taraweeh.

Short vs Long Dua Qunoot: Key Differences

Both versions are considered valid within Islamic practice, and the difference largely comes down to regional custom, madhab preference, and the setting (individual prayer vs. congregational prayer led by an imam).

Qunoot al-Nazilah: A Special Category

It’s worth briefly distinguishing regular Dua Qunoot from Qunoot al-Nazilah – a special supplication recited during times of severe hardship, calamity, war, or crisis affecting the Muslim community. Unlike the regular Witr qunoot, Qunoot al-Nazilah:

  • Can be recited in any of the five daily prayers, not just Witr
  • Is typically recited after rising from ruku in the final rak’ah
  • Often includes specific supplications relevant to the crisis at hand (e.g., praying for the oppressed, for safety, for relief from hardship)

This is a separate practice from the everyday Dua Qunoot discussed above, though both share the same root concept of standing in humble supplication.

Which Version Should You Recite?

There is no single “correct” answer – this often depends on:

  1. Your madhab or regional tradition – if you follow a particular school of thought or community practice, it’s generally best to follow what’s taught by your local scholars or mosque.
  2. The setting – many people use the short version for personal, individual Witr prayer and reserve the long version (or a combination) for congregational prayer, especially during Ramadan.
  3. Personal comfort and memorization – beginners are often encouraged to start with the short, well-authenticated version before gradually learning the longer one if desired.

Ultimately, both the short and long Dua Qunoot are rooted in authentic Islamic tradition, and reciting either with sincerity and understanding is what matters most – not the length.

Tips for Memorizing Dua Qunoot

  • Break it into small phrases – memorize a few words at a time rather than the whole dua at once.
  • Understand the meaning – knowing what you’re saying makes memorization more meaningful and long-lasting.
  • Practice during non-prayer times first – repeat it during the day before trying to recite it confidently in Witr.
  • Listen to correct recitation – hearing a qualified reciter helps with pronunciation and rhythm.
  • Be consistent – reciting it nightly during Witr naturally reinforces memorization over time.

Final Thoughts

The existence of both short and long versions of Dua Qunoot reflects the richness and flexibility within Islamic scholarly tradition – not confusion or contradiction. Whether you recite the short, widely-authenticated version taught to Hasan ibn Ali, or the longer version common in congregational and Ramadan prayers, what matters most is approaching it with sincerity, humility, and a genuine desire to seek Allah’s guidance and mercy.

Take time to learn the version most relevant to your community and tradition, understand its meaning, and let it become a consistent, heartfelt part of your Witr prayer.

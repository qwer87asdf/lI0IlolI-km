When Was the Quran Written? A Complete Timeline From Revelation to Compilation

Hasan · 26 Jul 2026 · 6 min read

For over 1,400 years, the Quran has remained the central text of Islam, guiding the beliefs, practices, and daily lives of more than a billion Muslims worldwide. But one question that comes up often-especially among new Muslims, students of religion, and curious non-Muslims-is: when was the Quran actually written?

The answer isn’t a single date. It’s a journey-one that spans revelation, memorization, oral transmission, and eventual compilation into the written text we know today. This article breaks down that entire process in a clear, chronological timeline.

The Quran Was Revealed, Not “Written” All at Once

Before diving into the timeline, it’s important to understand a key distinction: the Quran was not authored in the way a book is typically written. Muslims believe it is the literal word of God (Allah), revealed to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ through the angel Jibril (Gabriel) over a period of approximately 23 years.

This revelation happened gradually-in response to events, questions, and circumstances the early Muslim community faced-rather than being delivered as one complete manuscript.

Complete Timeline: From First Revelation to Final Compilation

610 CE-The First Revelation in the Cave of Hira

The story begins when Muhammad ﷺ, then 40 years old, was meditating in the Cave of Hira, located on Jabal al-Nour (“Mountain of Light”) near Mecca. It was here that the Angel Jibril appeared to him and delivered the first verses of the Quran, found in Surah Al-Alaq (96:1-5), beginning with the word “Iqra” (“Read” or “Recite”).

This moment marks the beginning of Prophethood and the start of the Quran’s revelation.

610–622 CE-The Meccan Period

For the next 13 years, while still in Mecca, the Prophet ﷺ continued receiving revelations. These early chapters (surahs) tend to be shorter, poetic in style, and focus heavily on core beliefs: the Oneness of God (Tawheed), the Day of Judgment, moral conduct, and stories of earlier prophets.

622 CE-The Hijrah (Migration to Medina)

A turning point occurs with the Hijrah-the migration of the Prophet ﷺ and his followers from Mecca to Medina. This event is so significant that it marks the start of the Islamic (Hijri) calendar.

622–632 CE-The Medinan Period

Once in Medina, the nature of the revelations shifted. Medinan surahs tend to be longer and more detailed, addressing social laws, family matters, governance, warfare ethics, and community organization-reflecting the new responsibilities of building an actual Muslim society and state.

During the Prophet’s Lifetime-Oral Preservation and Early Writing

Throughout these 23 years, the Quran was preserved in two parallel ways:

  1. Memorization (Hifz): Companions of the Prophet ﷺ memorized the verses as they were revealed. Many companions, known as Huffaz (memorizers), could recite large portions-or the entire Quran-from memory.
  2. Written Recording: The Prophet ﷺ appointed scribes, most notably Zayd ibn Thabit, to write down verses as they were revealed. These were recorded on whatever materials were available at the time-parchment, flat stones, palm leaves, leather, and even animal bones and shoulder blades.

Importantly, this dual system-memorization plus writing-meant the Quran was never dependent on a single copy or a single person’s memory. This redundancy became crucial in the years following the Prophet’s death.

632 CE-The Death of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ

By the time of his death, the entire Quran had been revealed and existed in two forms: memorized by numerous companions and written down in scattered pieces by various scribes. However, it had not yet been compiled into a single, bound book.

The Compilation of the Quran Into a Single Text

632–634 CE-Caliph Abu Bakr’s Compilation

After the Prophet’s death, Abu Bakr became the first Caliph (leader of the Muslim community). During his rule, a serious challenge arose: the Battle of Yamama, in which a significant number of Huffaz (Quran memorizers) were killed.

This loss alarmed the companion Umar ibn al-Khattab, who urged Abu Bakr to organize a formal, complete written compilation of the Quran to safeguard it against loss. Abu Bakr assigned this task to Zayd ibn Thabit, who had been one of the Prophet’s primary scribes.

Zayd meticulously cross-referenced written fragments with the memories of multiple Huffaz to compile a single, authenticated manuscript. This became known as the Mushaf-the first complete written copy of the Quran as one unified text.

644–656 CE-Caliph Uthman ibn Affan’s Standardization

As Islam spread rapidly beyond Arabia into Persia, Syria, Egypt, and beyond, regional dialectical differences in recitation began causing confusion and, in some cases, disputes among new Muslim communities about the “correct” pronunciation and reading of the Quran.

To resolve this, the third Caliph, Uthman ibn Affan, ordered the creation of standardized copies of the Quran based on Abu Bakr’s original Mushaf. He formed a committee-again led by Zayd ibn Thabit-to produce several official copies written in the Quraysh dialect (the Prophet’s own dialect), which was considered the most authoritative.

These standardized copies, known as the Uthmanic codices, were sent to major Islamic centers such as Mecca, Kufa, Basra, and Damascus. All other variant manuscripts were ordered to be destroyed to prevent future discrepancies.

This Uthmanic text became the single, standardized version of the Quran that is used by Muslims worldwide today-essentially unchanged for nearly 1,400 years.

Why This Timeline Matters

Understanding “When Was the Quran Written” is a common misconception-that the Quran was written like an ordinary book, by one person, at one point in time. In reality, it reflects a careful, deliberate process of revelation over 23 years, followed by preservation through both memory and writing, and finally, formal compilation to ensure its accuracy would endure for generations.

Muslims believe this process, combined with the unbroken chain of memorization (Hifz) that continues to this day, is part of what has preserved the Quran’s text unchanged since the 7th century-a claim rooted in the Quranic verse:

“Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder [the Quran], and indeed, We will be its guardian.” (Surah Al-Hijr, 15:9)

Final Thoughts

The Quran’s journey-from a single revelation in a quiet cave outside Mecca to a fully standardized text distributed across a growing Islamic world-is a story of preservation, dedication, and continuity. While the revelation itself spanned 23 years (610–632 CE), the process of transforming it into the written, compiled text we recognize today took a few more decades, concluding under Caliph Uthman around 650 CE.

For Muslims, this history isn’t just academic-it’s a testament to the care taken to protect the words believed to be directly from God, ensuring they remain accessible and unaltered for every generation that follows.
